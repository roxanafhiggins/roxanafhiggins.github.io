Making a job profile worth finishing

A German job marketplace where the profile is the application. I rebuilt it, and gave people a reason to finish it.

What this project was

About the project

Workwise is a job marketplace in Germany. Candidates keep one profile and apply with it, no cover letters. Over 2,000 companies read that profile when someone applies, and search it when they go looking for people themselves. It is not a settings page. It is the product.

A CV cannot carry that. It goes out of date, it says nothing about what someone wants next, and you cannot compare it across thousands of people.

So I rebuilt structured input in both places it happens, the signup wizard and the profile page, then added gamification across the job search flow: a completion score on the candidate's own photo, and prompts where intent is already high. The score belongs to the candidate, not the platform. It never blocks the flow it sits in.

What I did

My role

I owned the work end to end, from research and structure to flows, edge cases and shipped UI.

I built the input components and worked with the wider design team to align them with the design system, so the same patterns run across B2C, B2B, the signup flow and Workwise staff products.

My contributions

  • Sessions with colleagues close to the target group, talent managers above all, run on every round.
  • Rebuilt profile page: skills, languages and levels, experience, CV, personal details.
  • A new section for what candidates want, including an actively looking toggle.
  • Input components, editing states and feedback, reused in the signup wizard and on the employer side.
  • Completion percentage in the account menu, and the prompt card inside job search.

Challenges

Nobody wants to fill in a profile. People are signed up to several job sites at once, they do not feel any ownership over any single profile, and filling one in is boring work with no immediate reward. So they skip fields, or they stop halfway, or they never come back to update the one they made two years ago.

That matters because the profile does two jobs. When a candidate applies, the company reads what they have done and what they are looking for, and decides from that. And companies search profiles directly, so a candidate can be found without applying to anything at all. A thin or two-year-old profile fails at both.

What research gave me

I could not talk to candidates, so I sat with the people closest to them. I booked sessions in the office with colleagues who are not technical and who deal with candidates all day, talent managers above all. They are close enough to the target group to behave like it.

Each person got one specific task and no help from me. I watched them do it and wrote down every point where they stopped, guessed, or asked what something meant. I ran this on every round, not once at the start.

Afterwards I sorted the notes into four piles, and the piles set the order of the work.

  • Usability issuesWhere the interface itself got in the way.
  • Copy feedbackWording that did not land, or asked for something people did not understand.
  • NavigationPoints where people could not tell where to go next.
  • Motivational dropoffThe moments where people stopped caring enough to carry on.

The sessions were not filmed and the notes are not a deliverable, so there is nothing to show here.

The structural finding: what you have done and what you want are two different questions. The old page mixed them, which is part of why it felt arbitrary. Separating them let each part be paced on its own, and gave the new intent section a place of its own instead of being buried.

What I shipped

Four parts of one job: the page itself, a new section for what people want, the components other teams now reuse, and the reminder that brings people back.

  • Part 1

    Easy to fill in

    The page rebuilt around entering information rather than displaying it. Mobile first.

  • Part 2

    What you want

    A new section for role, hours, salary, location, and whether you are actively looking.

  • Part 3

    Shared components

    Inputs, editing states and feedback built once, used by the wizard and the employer side.

  • Part 4

    A reason to come back

    Completion on your own photo, and a prompt inside the job list where people already are.

Part 1 of 4

Easy to fill in

I rebuilt the page around entering information rather than displaying it, mobile first, so skills, languages and their levels, experience, CV and personal details could be added and edited without friction. Most candidates do this on a phone, in a few minutes, between other things.

Part 2 of 4

Ask what people want, not just what they have done

This part did not exist before. Candidates now say what role they are after, full time or part time, salary, location, and whether they are actively looking, which is a simple toggle.

That is the information a company most wants to see, and the platform never used to collect it.

Part 3 of 4

Built as components other teams could use

Skill and language level selection, editing and adding states, and the small feedback moments that confirm information has been saved were built once, with rules and styling documented, then handed over.

They are used in the signup wizard and on the employer side, not just here.

Part 4 of 4

Put the reminder where people already are

Nobody opens their profile page in order to improve their profile. The card sits inside the job list, shaped like the job cards around it, so it belongs in the scroll rather than interrupting it.

The copy does not promise a better application, because writing a better application is more work. It promises being found by companies who are already searching, which is something that happens to you rather than something you have to do. The button says check your profile, not complete it, which is a smaller thing to agree to.

And the completion percentage is attached to the candidate's own photo, so it reads as how complete you are, rather than what the platform still wants from you.

The hardest call

I did not force it. The straightforward way to get complete profiles is to demand them, block the application until the profile is done. That would have worked on paper and lost the people who leave halfway.

I chose the slower route. Let people finish a usable profile quickly and earn the rest back over time, which means accepting that a real share of profiles are incomplete on day one. That only holds up because the prompts exist. Without them it is just a flow that gives up early.

CONFIRM whether gating was genuinely discussed, or whether this is reconstruction after the fact.

What I would do differently

I would have measured it. Two things would have settled the argument: where people drop out of the flow, and how many applications arrive with the intent fields filled in.

Without those I can defend the thinking but not prove the result. That is the part of this project I would not repeat.
